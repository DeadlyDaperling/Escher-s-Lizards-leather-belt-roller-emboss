# Escher-s-Lizards-leather-belt-roller-emboss
This project contains the tools and steps to create an Escher's Lizards roller emboss pattern for a leather belt

Printer: Bambu Lab P1S
Filament: PLA or PLA tough +
Wall loops: 4
Infill: 75%
Infill pattern: grid
Supports: none

1. Finding the base shape in the original lizard. The starting point was a DXF of a single Escher style lizard (outline plus internal detail lines). I measured its outline and found it is built on a nearly regular hexagon. Its corners, which I’ll call A through F, include three exact 120° corners (B, D and F). The other three measure about 124°, 112° and 124°, and each set of three corners totals 360°, which is what lets tiles fit together at a corner. Escher’s trick here is a variation on the usual cut-and-slide method. Instead of sliding a bump to the opposite side, each piece is swung 120° around a corner onto the adjacent side. The limb cut into side AB is the same shape rotated about B onto side BC, and the same is done around D (sides CD and DE) and around F (sides EF and FA). Wherever the lizard has a bump, its neighbour has a matching notch, and the area is preserved. The three pivot corners form an equilateral triangle with sides of 98.2 units. Rotating copies of the lizard about those points generated the three orientations, 120° apart, and translating them generated a seamless tessellation from the single DXF lizard.
2. The repeat vectors. The pattern repeats by translation. I’ll call the two basic repeat vectors t₁ and t₂ (the “t” is for translation). Sliding the whole tessellation by t₁ or t₂ puts every lizard exactly on another lizard of the same orientation. Both vectors are about 170.1 units long (98.2 × √3) and point 120° apart. Every other exact repeat is a whole-number combination of them: a·t₁ + b·t₂, where a is the number of steps along t₁ and b is the number of steps along t₂. They must be whole numbers, because a partial step would not land on an identical copy.
3. Fitting the pattern to the unwrapped roller. The 1.95" roller unrolls to a flat rectangle 6.126" wide (the circumference, π × 1.95) by 1.5" tall (the roller length). The pattern wraps seamlessly only if the left and right edges of that rectangle match, which means the width must equal one of the repeat combinations, w = a·t₁ + b·t₂. The cutting trick doesn’t change the repeat vectors, so they come from the base hexagon and its pivot points, and that is why only certain angles work.
Straight (0°) versions: The width was a whole number of repeats along t₁ (for example (a, b) = (4, 0), meaning 4 steps along t₁ and none along t₂), and the pattern was scaled so that length equalled 6.126".
Angled versions: The width used both vectors. By the law of cosines (t₁ and t₂ are 120° apart), the wrap vector’s length is |w| = 170.1 × √(a² − ab + b²), and it tilts away from t₁ by atan2(√3·b/2, a − b/2). For example, (a, b) = (5, 1) gives |w| ≈ 779.5 units and a tilt of about 11°. I rotated the pattern by minus that angle so w lay horizontally, then scaled it so that w measured exactly 6.126" (about 0.00786" per unit). A lizard is about 150 units long, so the lizards come out about 1.18" long. I then cut out a 6.126" × 1.5" window. Because the width is a repeat vector, the tiling at the left and right edges is identical, so the seam closes while the lizards sit tilted. The pair (5, 2) works the same way and gives a tilt of about 23° with lizards about 1.24" long. Any pair of whole numbers works, and each one gives its own tilt and lizard size. An angle that is not one of these combinations leaves a mismatch at the seam.
4. Preparing the geometry for Onshape. For the outline versions I kept only the lizard outlines and thickened them to 0.05" ridges, as closed shapes. For the solid-group versions I also filled one orientation group. For the three-height version, each orientation group went into its own DXF, with the tall group at 0.045", the mid group at 0.0225", and the third group left flat.
5. Making the die in Onshape. The roller blank (1.95" diameter, 1.01" keyed bore, 1.5" long) was extruded, and the DXF was imported into a sketch in inches. The sketch was wrapped around the outer cylinder with the Wrap feature, then given its raised height (0.045", or 0.0225" for the mid group) to form the embossing die.
